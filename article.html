Why Does Dark Mode Even Exist?

📅 Oct 20, 2026  •  ⏱️ 4 min read  •  ✍️ The Daily Byte Team

Open your phone at night and you will probably be greeted by a bright white screen that feels like staring at a torch. That is exactly the problem dark mode tries to solve. But where did it come from, and is it really better?

A Quick History

Believe it or not, dark mode is older than light mode. The earliest computer monitors displayed glowing green or white text on a black background, because that was simply how the technology worked. When Windows and Mac introduced paper-like white screens in the 1980s, it felt natural for office work, and dark interfaces slowly disappeared. Now they are back, and everyone loves them.

Does It Save Battery?

Yes, but only on certain screens. On OLED and AMOLED displays, every pixel makes its own light, so a black pixel is simply switched off. Dark mode can therefore save a good amount of battery on these phones. On older LCD screens, the backlight stays on anyway, so the savings are very small.

Is It Better for Your Eyes?

This is where people get confused. Dark mode reduces glare and feels more comfortable in dim rooms, which is why many people read better with it at night. However, in bright daylight, dark text on a light background is usually easier to read. So neither mode is "best" for everyone. It depends on where you are and what you prefer.

Quick Tips

  • Use dark mode at night or in low-light rooms.
  • Use light mode in bright places like outdoors or a sunny classroom.
  • Take a 20-second break every 20 minutes, whichever mode you use.

Final Thoughts

Dark mode is not magic, but it is a great option to have. The best part is that you control it. Just click the button at the top of this page and see for yourself!

5 Study Habits That Actually Work

📅 Oct 22, 2026  •  ⏱️ 5 min read  •  ✍️ The Daily Byte Team

We have all pulled the late-night "read everything again" session before exams, only to forget half of it the next morning. The good news is that learning has a few simple rules. Follow these five habits and you will study smarter, not longer.

1. Test Yourself, Don't Just Re-read

Re-reading feels productive, but your brain is just recognizing the words. Close the book and try to write or say what you remember. This is called active recall, and it is one of the strongest ways to build memory.

2. Space It Out

Studying one topic for 5 hours in one day is far less effective than studying it for 1 hour a day across 5 days. Spaced repetition gives your brain time to store information in long-term memory.

3. Teach It to Someone (or Something)

Try explaining a concept to a friend, or even to your wall. If you get stuck, you have just found the part you do not understand yet. Simple analogies help a lot here. For example, think of RAM as your desk and the hard disk as your cupboard.

4. Use Short Focus Sessions

Try the Pomodoro method: study for 25 minutes, then take a 5-minute break. After four rounds, take a longer break. Short sprints keep your mind fresh and stop you from zoning out.

5. Sleep Is Part of Studying

Your brain organizes what you learned while you sleep. Skipping sleep to study more usually backfires. Aim for a regular sleep schedule, especially in the days before exams.

Final Thoughts

You do not need to do everything at once. Pick one habit this week, try it, and see how it feels. Small changes add up fast. All the best for your exams!
